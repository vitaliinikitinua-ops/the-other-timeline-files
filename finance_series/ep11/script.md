## 1:15-2:30 · TIMELINE A — THE FIRST CLICK
[Store. Cashier with a nametag, grinning, scanner beeps. Price tag "$200" shrinks into "4 x $50" with a cute pop.]

VO: Timeline A. The jacket is two hundred dollars. Too much for one day. But the Cashier leans in.
CASHIER (bubble): "Or just fifty today!"
VO: Fifty. Fifty is nothing. Fifty is a dinner. Click. Here's the trick, and it's not Karl being stupid. Your brain doesn't feel two hundred dollars. It feels fifty. Splitting a price into four makes paying hurt about four times less. That's not a bug. That's the product. Stores pay these apps a fee on every sale because people who split the price buy more. One study from the U.S. National Bureau of Economic Research found that after people start using buy now, pay later, their spending goes up and stays up, around sixty dollars a week.

[Counter top-right: "PLANS: 1 · $200".]

## 2:30-3:45 · TIMELINE A — STACKING
[Fast montage, punch-ins: headphones, sneakers, a smart speaker, a gaming chair, a "gift for mom" that becomes two. Each time the same pop: "4 x $50". Counter ticks: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.]

VO: Then the headphones. Fifty today. The sneakers. Fifty today. A speaker he didn't know he needed. Fifty today. None of these feel big, because none of them are big. Alone. By Cyber Monday, Karl has six plans in four different apps. And he's not unusual. The U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau looked at real borrowers: sixty-three percent had several of these loans open at the same time. A third used more than one app. And it peaks exactly now, during the holidays. Last holiday season Americans put about twenty billion dollars on buy now, pay later online.

[Counter: "PLANS: 6". Then, one by one, all six tags flip to show the full price: $200 x 6.]

VO: Remember your guess? Here's the first real number. Six plans of two hundred. Karl just spent one thousand two hundred dollars. Three times his list.

[Tag: "STILL THINK $____?"]

## 3:45-4:30 · THE PRESSURE MACHINE (myths)
[Timers everywhere: "ENDS IN 00:09:59", "ONLY 2 LEFT", confetti on "Pay in 4". The Cashier spins a wheel labelled "URGENCY".]

VO: And look at everything around that button. A timer. "Only two left." A big green "zero interest". All of it is built to make you decide in seconds, not days. Now the two myths Karl believes. Myth one: "It's interest-free, so it's free." It's free only if every single payment is on time, and only if you would have bought it anyway. Forty percent off something you didn't plan to buy isn't a saving. It's sixty percent spent. Myth two: "It's not real debt." It is a loan. And for years most of these loans didn't even show up on credit reports. Which means nobody saw the full stack. Not the banks, not the apps. Not even Karl.

## 4:30-5:30 · TIMELINE B — FULL PRICE OUT LOUD
[Same store, teal light. Same Cashier, same grin: "Or just fifty today!"]

VO: Timeline B. Same jacket, same Cashier, same button. But this Karl has one rule. Before he pays, he says the full price out loud. "Two hundred dollars." It sounds silly. It works, because now his brain hears the real number. Then a second question: would I pay two hundred in cash, today? For the jacket, yes. He planned it. He pays with his debit card. Done.
[Headphones appear. Karl: "One hundred ninety-nine dollars." Pause. He puts them back in the cart and closes the app.]
VO: For the headphones he says it out loud and hears how it sounds. So he leaves them in the cart for forty-eight hours. Black Friday prices aren't the last sale of the year. Most of them come back before Christmas. Two days later, he doesn't even remember them. Timeline B spends four hundred dollars. The list. Nothing else.

[Split screen: A "$1,200" stack of boxes vs B "$400" two boxes.]

## 5:30-6:15 · HOURS OF LIFE
[Price tags morph into clocks. "$1,200 = 32 HOURS". A calendar week, four workdays fill with red.]

VO: Let's translate this into something money hides: time. The average American earns about thirty-eight dollars an hour before tax. So Timeline A's twelve hundred dollars is about thirty-two hours of work. Four full days of Karl's life, for things he mostly didn't plan to buy. Timeline B's extra, the stuff he didn't buy, is eight hundred dollars. Twenty-one hours he gets to keep.

## 6:15-7:00 · TIMELINE A — DECEMBER
[Phone calendar: Dec 11 "$300", Dec 25 "$300" next to a Christmas tree, Jan 8 "$300". Six apps' icons buzzing.]

VO: Back to Timeline A. Every two weeks, six payments land on the same days. Three hundred dollars. On December eleventh. On Christmas day. On January eighth, the same week as rent. And Karl is not the only one struggling. In a LendingTree survey, forty-one percent of people who use these loans paid late at least once in a year. The latest version says forty-seven. Late fees are up to seven or eight dollars a time, and a missed payment can freeze the app. So Karl does what feels smart.

[Teal. Same calendar, January 8th: just "RENT", paid, green tick. Karl eating noodles, relaxed, a little bored.]

VO: Meanwhile, Timeline B. January eighth. Rent. That's it. No bubbles, no buzzing apps. Honestly, it's a boring January. Karl B didn't get the speaker or the gaming chair, and for about two days he felt like he lost Black Friday. But his paycheck is his again. And the eight hundred dollars he didn't spend is still sitting there, waiting for a decision.

## 7:30-8:05 · TWIST
[Close-up: payment screen, "Pay with: credit card". Karl taps. Banker-style red "21%" stamps the screen. Music drops.]

VO: He pays each fifty-dollar payment with his credit card. No late fees, no freezes. Problem solved, right? Here's the twist. The zero-interest loan just moved onto a card that charges about twenty-one percent a year. That's the U.S. average on cards that pay interest, August 2026. By January, the whole twelve hundred dollars is sitting on that card. And Karl pays the minimum, because the minimum is what the app suggests.

## 8:05-8:45 · REVEAL (~85%)
[Chart: line climbing year by year, 2027 ... 2033. Label "MINIMUM PAYMENTS". At the end, a receipt prints out of the floor, longer and longer. Big number.]

VO: So, your guess. How much did Karl really spend? At the minimum payment it takes him about seven years to clear the card. Seven years for a Black Friday. He pays about one thousand seventy dollars in interest. Total: about two thousand two hundred seventy dollars. And remember the full price, before the forty percent off? Two thousand. Karl paid more than full price for a sale.

[Big: "-40% = $2,270 > $2,000". Karl's jaw drops; the Cashier's grin freezes.]

VO: And Timeline B? If he had put those eight hundred unspent dollars into the S&P 500 ten years ago, they'd be worth about two thousand eight hundred fifty today. That's history, not a promise. But the gap is real: one Karl has a seven-year receipt, the other has money.

## 8:45-9:40 · HONEST PART + 3 RULES
[Calm teal room. Three cards slide in.]

VO: To be fair: buy now, pay later is not evil. If you pay every installment on time, from money you already have, for something you'd buy anyway, it really is zero interest. Some people use it well. The trap is the stack, and the card behind it. And the trap is designed for exactly the moment you're tired, it's late, and the timer says nine minutes. So three rules. Simple enough to remember at the checkout.
Rule one: say the full price out loud. If you wouldn't pay it in cash today, don't split it.
Rule two: one plan at a time. Never for groceries or bills. If you need a second plan, you can't afford the first.
Rule three: never pay a buy-now-pay-later loan with a credit card. If you already have a stack, put every date in one calendar, pay the smallest first, and stop opening new ones.
This is education, not financial advice.
