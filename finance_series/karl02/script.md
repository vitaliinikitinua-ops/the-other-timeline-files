# Karl #02 — What If Every "Pay in 4" Button Showed You the Real Price?
Remake of ep11 (Black Friday / BNPL) in the new formula (`karl-2d-template`, 3D toon engine `engine3d/toon`).
Karl **23**, first office job, turquoise hoodie, no cap. Cast: Mom, Dad, Mia (16), Grandma Rose, Max, Lena (coworker).
Sets: bedroom (night), store/mall, office, kitchen, mall atrium with stage (climax).
Power visual: when Karl looks at a price or a "Pay in 4" button, an AMBER ring pings and the button text
flips to the real price; our signature RECEIPT prints the details (full price / hours of his life / if late).
Narration: 2nd person, present tense, deadpan, ~190 wpm. Target 9.5-10.5 min. All money rounded (exact in BRIEF).

Numbers (computed in `karl02/karl02_numbers.py`, sources in the description):
- EXAMPLE wage: Karl takes home $20 an hour -> $120 = 6 h, $400 = 20 h, $600 = 30 h, $1,200 = 60 h, $18 = ~1 h, $6 = 18 min.
- EXAMPLE Dad's truck: $499 x 72 months = ~$36,000. Mia: $240 = 30 h of babysitting at $8/h (example).
- EXAMPLE Max's cart: $2,000 full price, -40% = $1,200 = 6 plans x $200 = 24 payments of $50.
- Card APR 21.19% (FRED TERMCBCCALLNS, Aug 2026) -> "about 21%". $1,200 at minimum payments (1% + interest, min $25)
  -> 87 months (~7 years), ~$1,070 interest, ~$2,270 total = MORE than the $2,000 full price.
- F1 CFPB (Jan 2025): 63% of BNPL borrowers had more than one loan at the same time -> "about six in ten".
- F2 LendingTree (2025 survey): 41% of BNPL users paid late in the past year; 2026 update: 47% -> "four in ten... now almost half".
- F3 Adobe (Jan 2026): ~$20 billion spent with BNPL in the 2025 holiday season.

## 0:00 COLD OPEN — the button at 11:58
[Bedroom, night, blue light. Karl in bed, phone over his face. Clock 11:58. Ad: headphones, "-50%", "PAY IN 4: JUST $30 TODAY".]
It's 11:58 on Black Friday night.
You're in bed, scrolling, the way you do.
Wireless headphones. Fifty percent off. Two minutes left.
And the friendly little button: "Pay in 4. Just thirty dollars today."
Thirty dollars. That's nothing. That's a pizza.
Your thumb is already moving.
Then the button flickers.
[Amber ring. Button text flips: "$120 · 6 HOURS OF YOUR LIFE".]
"One hundred twenty dollars. Six hours of your life."
You blink. You refresh.
The button flickers again.
[Flip: "YOU ALREADY OWN THESE".]
"You already own these."
You look at your desk.
The same headphones. Last year's Black Friday. Still in the box.
Still paying themselves off.
[Receipt pops from the box: "HEADPHONES / PAYMENT 3 OF 4".]
You put the phone down. 11:59. The sale ends. Nothing happens.
It's the first time nothing happening feels like winning.

## 0:42 PREMISE
Most people see "Pay in 4."
You see the real price.
Every price tag. Every "only today". Every "just thirty dollars".
For you, it shows the whole number, the hours of your life it costs, and what it turns into if one payment slips.
You're twenty-three. First real job. First real paycheck. About twenty dollars an hour, after tax.
[Small label: "EXAMPLE".]
And now every button in the world tells you the truth.

## 1:05 "At first, it's amazing."
At first, it's amazing.
Saturday morning, the mall. A giant sign: "Forty percent off. Today only!"
Under it, for you, in small amber letters: "Also today only last Tuesday."
You laugh out loud. A security guard looks at you.
Your best friend Max is already in line with a gaming chair.
"Just a hundred today, bro."
Above his button, you see four hundred dollars. Twenty hours. His fourth plan this month.
You say nothing. He's happy. It's a nice chair.
At home, Mom is about to buy a blender. "Only twenty-two fifty, four times!"
The real price glows over her phone: "Ninety dollars. You have a blender."
She looks at the blender on the counter. She laughs so hard she has to sit down.
You just saved Mom ninety dollars. She pays you in leftover lasagna.
Your little sister Mia wants two pairs of sneakers. "Pay in four, it's basically free."
You tell her what you see. "Two hundred forty dollars. Thirty hours of babysitting."
She buys one pair. She hates you for one whole day. Then she writes the number in her notebook.
Dad wants a new truck. The salesman smiles. "Only four ninety-nine a month."
Above the smile, you see it. Seventy-two payments. About thirty-six thousand dollars.
Dad keeps his old truck. He names it "Thirty-Six Thousand".
Grandma Rose pays for everything in cash, from little envelopes.
Above Grandma's price tags, the real price is exactly the same as the price.
She's the only person in town like that.
"Don't look so surprised," she says. "I'm old, not new."
And at work, there's Lena.
Lena sits two desks away and fixes the printer nobody else can fix.
At the coffee cart, you buy two coffees. Six dollars each.
The real price for hers says: "Six dollars. Worth it."
It's the most romantic receipt you have ever seen.

## 2:05 [subscribe pill]
## 2:10 "Then it gets bigger."
Then it gets bigger.
Max films you at the mall, reading real prices out loud.
"This TV. Six hundred dollars, thirty hours, eighteen months of payments."
Max posts it. By Monday, it has two million views.
People call you "the Real Price Guy".
Strangers stop you in the street and hold up their phones.
"What does mine say?"
A store manager follows you through the aisles with a sign: "No real prices past this point."
Two checkout apps block your number. One sends you a cake. The cake says "Pay in 4".
The news calls. You read real prices on live TV. A gold watch. A sofa. A wedding dress.
The anchor laughs. Then she checks her own phone. Then she stops laughing.
And the more buttons you read, the more you see how big this is.
Last holiday season, Americans spent about twenty billion dollars with buy now, pay later.
[Receipt: "HOLIDAY 2025 / BNPL / ~$20 BILLION / ADOBE".]
Twenty billion. Four easy payments at a time.
Then a golden envelope arrives.
The biggest mall in the state is opening its Christmas mega sale.
Ten thousand people. Midnight. Live on TV.
And they want you to press the very first "Pay in 4" button. On stage.
They'll pay you five thousand dollars to do it.
You say yes. Five thousand is a lot of real price.

## 3:40 "But..." — THE TURN
But you can't turn it off.
On your first real date with Lena, the menu glows.
Pasta: eighteen dollars, almost one hour. Dessert: forty minutes.
You don't say it out loud. You don't have to. You stop ordering.
"Are you okay?" Lena asks. "You're reading the menu like it's a contract."
"It kind of is," you say. She doesn't laugh.
Then there's Max.
Max isn't buying one thing. Max is buying everything.
A two-thousand-dollar cart. Forty percent off. Twelve hundred dollars.
Split into six plans. Twenty-four little payments of fifty dollars.
Every one of them looks tiny. Nobody sees the total. Except you.
[Max's phone: six bubbles "$50"; over them one big amber number "$1,200 · 60 HOURS".]
And Max is not weird.
The U.S. consumer finance bureau found that about six in ten people who use buy now, pay later had more than one loan at the same time.
[Receipt: "MORE THAN ONE BNPL LOAN AT ONCE / ~6 IN 10 / CFPB 2025".]
Here's the trick, and it's not Max being stupid.
Your brain doesn't feel twelve hundred dollars. It feels fifty.
Split a price into four and paying just hurts less.
That's not a bug. That's the product.
Stores pay these apps a fee on every sale, because people who split the price buy more.
At Max's birthday, in front of everybody, he says, "Relax. It's only fifty here, fifty there."
And you hear yourself say it.
"It's twelve hundred, Max. It's sixty hours of your life."
The room goes quiet.
Max puts down his cake.
"Must be nice," he says, "to see everything and still feel smarter than everybody."
He doesn't answer your texts that week.

## 5:00 THE COST
In January, all of Max's fifty-dollar payments land in the same two weeks as his rent.
He misses one. Then two.
He's not alone. In one big survey, about four in ten people who used buy now, pay later paid late in the past year.
This year, it's almost half.
[Receipt: "PAID LATE IN THE PAST YEAR / 41% -> 47% / LENDINGTREE".]
So Max does what a lot of people do. He moves the whole twelve hundred onto his credit card.
About twenty-one percent interest a year.
He pays the minimum. And above his card, you see the real price print. And print. And print.
[Long receipt, EXAMPLE label: "$1,200 ON A CARD / ~21% A YEAR / MINIMUM PAYMENTS / ~7 YEARS / TOTAL ~$2,270".]
Seven years.
About two thousand two hundred seventy dollars.
The forty percent off cost him more than the full price.
And it's not only Max.
At home, Mom's phone lights up on the kitchen table.
Three "Pay in 4" plans. Christmas gifts. One of them is for you.
Above your own present, it says: "One hundred sixty dollars. Two weeks of grocery money."
That night you sit on the stairs and hear Mom and Dad in the kitchen.
Dad says, "We can't keep doing this every December."
Mom says, "I just wanted it to feel like a normal Christmas."

## 6:10 LOW POINT
After that, everything has a price tag.
Your bed. Your lamp. The headphones in the box.
Friends stop inviting you to the mall. Lena says she needs a break from "the math".
Mia hides her notebook when you walk in.
You stop buying Lena coffee. Six dollars, you think. Eighteen minutes.
You walk to work the long way, around the mall.
You lie in the dark, and the whole room glows amber.
And you understand something.
Seeing the real price was never the hard part.
Saying it in a way people can hear is.
The mega sale is in three days.
They still want you to press the button.

## 6:55 CLIMAX — the mega sale
Midnight. Ten thousand people in one mall. Phones up. Carts full.
A giant red button on stage: "PAY IN 4."
The host hands you the microphone. "Real Price Guy! Press it!"
You look at the crowd. Ten thousand carts. Ten thousand real prices.
And you don't press it.
"I'm not here to tell you what to buy," you say.
"Some of you need that fridge. Some of you can pay in four and it'll be fine."
"But here's what the button doesn't say."
"Before you press 'Pay in 4,' say the full price out loud."
"Not the fifty. The whole thing."
"If you'd pay it in cash today, press it. If you wouldn't, it's not a deal. It's a loan."
The mall goes quiet. The host looks at his producer. The producer looks at the floor.
Then, somewhere at the back, a voice.
"Twelve hundred dollars!"
It's Max. Holding his phone up.
"Twelve hundred! And I'm paying it off!"
Somebody claps. Then somebody else says their number. "Three hundred and forty!"
Then Dad. "Thirty-six thousand! And I said no!"
Then Mom. "Four hundred eighty!" Then Grandma Rose, who has never owed anyone a dime: "Zero!"
And for one night, something strange happens.
[Amber rings spread from Karl over the whole crowd; every price tag in the mall flips at once.]
The power jumps off you and onto every price tag in the building.
For one night, everyone sees what you see.
Some people put things back. Some people buy them anyway, on purpose, out loud.
Nobody is ashamed. Everybody is counting.
[Receipt for the whole mall, printing: "SAID OUT LOUD: 10,000 / PUT BACK: A LOT / PAID ON PURPOSE: THE REST".]
The host never gets his button press.
They don't pay you the five thousand.
It's the best deal you've ever made.

## 8:40 MIRROR ENDING
One year later. 11:58 on Black Friday night.
You're in bed, scrolling.
Wireless headphones. Fifty percent off. "Pay in 4. Just thirty dollars today."
The button doesn't flicker anymore.
You don't need it to.
You say it out loud. "One hundred twenty dollars. Six hours."
Would you pay it in cash today? No.
You close the app. 11:59. Nothing happens. Still winning.
Max has one plan left, and a list of due dates on his fridge.
Mom gave everyone handmade gifts this Christmas. Dad cried at his. It was a mug.
Dad's old truck, Thirty-Six Thousand, still starts every morning. Mostly.
Mia bought her sneakers with babysitting money. She shows you the receipt. Proudly.
Grandma Rose gives you an envelope. Inside: twenty dollars and a note. "For the jar."
And at the coffee cart, you buy two coffees.
"Six dollars," you say out loud. "Worth it."
Lena raises an eyebrow. "Did you just price me?"
"Yeah," you say. "Worth it."
She laughs. It sounds like a printer that finally works.
Here's the one rule you keep:
Before any "Pay in 4," say the full price out loud. If you wouldn't pay it in cash today, it's not a deal. It's a loan.

## 9:50 QUESTION + END
So... would you want every "Pay in 4" button to show you the real price?
And what's the first thing you'd look at?
