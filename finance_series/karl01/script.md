# Karl #01 — What If You Could See Everyone's Bank Account?
Style: karl-2d-template (3D toon engine `engine3d/toon`). Karl 15-16, school + home. Cast: Mom, Dad, Mia, Grandma Rose, Max, Lena.
Power visual: a small dark rounded UI bar above every head with a balance (green = positive, red = negative), it
"pings" with a ring when Karl looks; our receipt pops out for details. Narration: 2nd person, present tense, deadpan,
~190 wpm. Target 9.5-10.5 min (~1,850 words). Numbers on screen rounded.

Real facts (sources in the description):
- F1 SHED 2025 (Federal Reserve, May 2026): 63% of adults would cover a $400 emergency with cash or equivalent -> ~4 in 10 would not.
- F2 Survey of Consumer Finances 2022 (Fed): median family net worth ~$193,000, mean ~$1.06 million (the average is pulled up by the very rich).
- F3 Agarwal, Mikhed, Scholnick (Philadelphia Fed WP 18-16 / Review of Financial Studies 2020): when someone wins the lottery, their close neighbours become MORE likely to go bankrupt (bigger prize, more bankruptcies) - they spend to keep up.

## 0:00 COLD OPEN — the sneakers
[Cafeteria. Max puts a shoebox on the table like a trophy.]
Max slams a shoebox on the cafeteria table.
"Limited edition. Two hundred dollars. My dad basically owns the mall."
You look at Max. Then you look a little higher.
Right above his head, floating like a video game health bar, there's a number.
Three dollars and twelve cents.
[Bar pings: $3.12 in red-orange. Receipt pops: "SNEAKERS $200 / PAY IN 4 / PAID: 1 of 4".]
And under it, a tiny receipt. "Pay in four. One of four paid."
Max catches you staring at the air above his head.
"What? Is there something on my hair?"
"No," you say. "Nice shoes."
Max nods like a king. Three dollars and twelve cents nods with him.

## 0:38 PREMISE
Most people look at faces.
You see balances.
Everyone you meet has a number floating over their head. What they really have. Today. Right now.

## 0:50 "At first, it's amazing."
At first, it's amazing.
Your little sister Mia swears she has no money to chip in for Mom's birthday cake.
Above her head: forty-three dollars.
"Check the piggy bank under your bed," you say.
She stares at you like you're a wizard. Then she pays.
At the corner store, the guy with the gold watch and the loud car has a red number. Minus nine thousand.
The quiet janitor at your school, Mr. Ortiz, who has worn the same jacket for ten years?
Almost four hundred thousand dollars.
[Receipt: "EXAMPLE: $200 / MONTH / 40 YEARS / ~6% A YEAR = ~$398K".]
He waves at you with his mop. You wave back. You've never respected a mop more.
Grandma Rose says she lives on "just her little pension."
Her number is so long it doesn't fit above her head. It wraps around her bun.
She winks. "Don't tell your father."
Mom asks if you want the cheap cereal or the good one.
Above her head, the number isn't big. It's fine. Just fine.
You pick the cheap one. She smiles like she passed a test she didn't know she was taking.
At the school store, a senior tries to sell you a "vintage" hoodie for sixty dollars.
His number is a hundred and twenty. He really needs the sixty.
You buy it anyway. It's the first time a number has made you kind.
Max, meanwhile, keeps buying things. Wireless earbuds. A gaming chair he shows you on video. Pizza for the whole table.
Every time, his number blinks a little lower. Every time, he looks a little happier.
Every time, you say nothing.
And then there's Lena.
Lena sits by the window in math and draws tiny planets in the margins.
You decide you will never look above Lena's head.
It's the most romantic decision you have ever made.

## 2:05 "Then it gets bigger."
Then it gets bigger.
At the mall, you stop looking at stores and start looking at people.
The nicest outfits have the smallest numbers.
A woman with four shopping bags: minus two thousand three hundred.
A man in a suit, buying a coffee with a credit card: minus sixty thousand.
You start a notebook. "Looks rich" in one column. "Is rich" in the other.
After one Saturday, the columns almost never match.
[Insert: notebook, two columns, ticks mostly mismatched.]
On TV, a guy who calls himself "the Millionaire Mindset" is selling a course for nine hundred ninety-seven dollars.
His number is red. His rented Lamborghini has its own number. Also red.
You go to a car dealership with Dad, just to look.
The salesman in the shiny suit says, "For a guy like you, sir, it's only four hundred a month."
Above the salesman's head, the number is great. Above every customer's head, it's going down.
His whole job is moving numbers from their heads to his.
You try the internet. It doesn't work through a screen. Only in real life, only in person.
So online, everyone is rich again.
And the more numbers you see, the more you notice one thing.
A lot of them are tiny.
In 2025, the Federal Reserve asked Americans what they would do if they suddenly had to pay four hundred dollars.
About four in ten couldn't cover it with cash.
[On screen: "$400 SURPRISE BILL / 4 IN 10 COULDN'T PAY IN CASH / FED SHED 2025".]
Four in ten. In the cafeteria, that's every other table.
You also learn that "average" lies.
If you add up everything American families own and divide it, the average family has about a million dollars.
But the family right in the middle? About a hundred and ninety thousand.
[Bars: MEAN ~$1.06M vs MEDIAN ~$193K, SCF 2022.]
A few giant numbers pull the average up. Most people never see them.
You see them now. Every day.

## 4:00 "But..." — THE TURN
But you can't stop seeing.
At dinner, Dad says, "Business is great, kiddo."
Above his head, a red number. Minus two thousand three hundred. And it's flashing.
[Receipt: "CARD MINIMUM DUE / RENT IN 4 DAYS".]
Mom looks at Dad. Dad looks at his plate.
Nobody looks at the flashing number, because nobody else can see it.
The next week, Dad buys you the new phone you asked for in October.
"Happy early birthday."
The number over his head gets redder.
You say thank you. It's the hardest thank you of your life.
At school, you make a mistake.
Max is bragging again, so you lean over and whisper, "Dude. Three dollars."
You meant it as a joke.
By lunch, the whole table knows.
By the end of the day, someone has written "$3.12" on Max's locker.
Max doesn't sit with you on Monday.
Or Tuesday.
You find him behind the gym and try to say sorry.
"You think I don't know my number?" he says. "Everybody at home knows it. That's why I bought the shoes."
He doesn't let you finish.
And it gets worse, because you can't turn it off.
You start sorting people. Rich. Fake. Broke. Fine.
You catch yourself being nicer to the kid with the big number, and shorter with the kid with the small one.
Lena notices.
"You keep looking over everyone's head," she says. "Like you're reading price tags."
"I'm not," you say.
She doesn't believe you. You don't believe you either.
And then you find out something about "keeping up".
Researchers looked at people whose neighbor won the lottery.
The bigger the prize next door, the more likely the neighbors were to go bankrupt.
They didn't win anything. They just tried to keep up.
[On screen: "NEIGHBOR WINS LOTTERY -> NEIGHBORS' BANKRUPTCY RISK GOES UP / PHILADELPHIA FED STUDY".]
Max wasn't lying because he's a liar.
Max was keeping up with you. With all of you.

## 6:10 LOW POINT
The class trip to the coast costs four hundred dollars.
The sign-up sheet goes around the room.
Above half the heads, the number is smaller than four hundred.
Nobody says it. Everybody just says, "Oh, I think I'm busy that weekend."
Max says he's busy. Mia says her friends are all busy.
And then, without meaning to, you look above Lena's head.
You see her number.
And then, for the first time, you see your own. It's floating above you in the window's reflection.
Eleven dollars.
[Mirror reflection: Karl, $11.]
You've been reading everyone else's numbers for months, and you never once looked at yours.
That night you sit on the stairs and hear Mom and Dad in the kitchen, whispering about the card.
Dad says, "I just didn't want him to feel poor."
Mom says, "He's not poor. We're just pretending."

## 7:20 CLIMAX — the assembly
On Friday, the school holds an assembly about the trip.
Eight hundred kids in one gym. Eight hundred numbers.
The principal asks if anyone has a question.
Your hand goes up before your brain can stop it.
You walk to the microphone.
"I can see everyone's bank account," you say.
Eight hundred kids laugh.
"I'm serious. I won't say anybody's number. But here's what I see."
"The newest shoes in this room have some of the smallest numbers."
"The quietest jackets have some of the biggest."
"And almost half of you, right now, couldn't pay four hundred dollars for this trip without asking someone."
The gym goes quiet.
"So here's my idea. Everyone puts in what they can. Anonymously. A box by the door. No names."
"And nobody says they're busy anymore."
Somewhere in the back, someone laughs, then stops.
The principal reaches for the microphone, then doesn't.
For a second, nothing happens.
Then Mr. Ortiz, the janitor, walks to the front, takes out his wallet, and drops something into the box.
[Ring of gold light around the box.]
Then Grandma Rose, who came to watch, does the same.
Then the kid with the gold sneakers. Then Lena.
Then Max, holding three dollars and twelve cents in coins.
[Receipt prints and prints: "TRIP FUND / 214 DONATIONS / $9,860 / EVERYONE GOES".]
By Monday, every kid who wants to go is going.
Nobody else can see the numbers. Only you.
And all over the gym, the red ones are going up.
Not because anyone got richer. Because nobody has to pretend anymore.

## 8:45 MIRROR ENDING
Back in the cafeteria, Max puts his old sneakers on the table.
"I returned them," he says. "Three payments left. My dad was so relieved he cried."
You don't look above his head.
You don't need to.
At dinner, Dad tells you the truth about the card.
You give him your eleven dollars. He laughs so hard he has to sit down.
Then, together, you make a plan. Same number, every Friday. Into a jar.
Mia slides two dollars from her piggy bank across the table. "For the jar."
Grandma Rose finally tells you her big secret. There isn't one.
"Same amount, every month, for fifty years," she says. "Boring is my superpower."
Lena sits down next to you with her planets.
"So," she says, "what's my number?"
"I don't know," you say. "I stopped looking."
That's a lie. But it's the best one you ever told.
[The bars above everyone fade. Karl's own number ticks up: $11 -> $21.]
Here is the one rule you keep:
Don't compare your inside to someone else's outside. Compare this month to last month.
Your number is going up. Slowly. It's the first number you've ever been proud of.

## 9:55 QUESTION + END
So... would you want to see everyone's bank account?
And whose number would you look at first?
